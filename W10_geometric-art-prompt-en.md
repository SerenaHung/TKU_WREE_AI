# 3:4 Art Poster — Photography and Abstract Geometry

Turn my uploaded photograph into a standalone, refined **3:4 portrait-format art poster**.

## 1. Overall Composition

Divide the canvas horizontally into two equal halves:

- **Top half (50%):** Preserve the authentic photographic character of the original image.
- **Bottom half (50%):** Extract the subject’s defining visual features and turn them into a recognizable abstract geometric symbol.

Make the relationship between the two halves clear so the viewer can see how the symbol derives from the photographed subject.

## 2. Top Half — Fine-Art Photography

Preserve the subject’s outline, proportions, pose, structure, natural light and shadow, and original color atmosphere.

Apply only subtle color correction. Aim for the finish of fine-art photography, an independent publication, or a carefully produced photography book.

Extend the background naturally if needed to fit the frame. Do not stretch, distort, or arbitrarily alter the subject’s features.

## 3. Bottom Half — Abstract Visual Symbol

Identify the visual features that make the subject recognizable:

- Its outline and geometric structure
- Its distinctive pose and proportions
- Its characteristic colors and shapes
- The mood it conveys and any relationships suggested by the scene

Distill these features into a single, self-contained, minimalist abstract logo that remains recognizable.

Build a distinct visual identity through geometric deconstruction, modular forms, linework, negative space, and stylization. Avoid tracing the entire subject.

Use these design references:

- Post-digital Graphic Design
- Deconstructed Swiss Design
- Generative Symbol System
- Diagrammatic Abstraction

Use a limited set of geometric modules, fine lines, arcs, grids, abstract nodes, and tiny index numbers to create a coherent visual language.

Create a sense of tension between order and imperfection, precision and handmade marks, digital and analog qualities, and rational structure and emotion.

**Make the symbol the sole focal point of the bottom half.** Center it or place it slightly off-center, leaving generous negative space.

Avoid elaborate decoration, clusters of icons, infographic-like layouts, and overly literal illustration.

## 4. Color System

Sample characteristic colors from the original photograph and organize them into a simple, cohesive palette.

Use warm white, off-white gray, or a muted neutral as the main background.

Use small accents of teal, blue, magenta, or orange where they serve a visual purpose and relate to the original photograph’s colors.

Use color to reinforce the visual identity. Avoid garish neon, overly saturated gradients, and cluttered color combinations.

## 5. Print Textures and Details

Incorporate subtle physical print effects:

- Photocopy (Xerox) texture
- Risograph grain
- Slight print misregistration
- Scanner noise
- Subtle paper texture

Retain slight edge imperfections, fine grain, and minor print irregularities to give the design the tactile quality of experimental graphic design.

Keep these textures restrained so the main symbol remains crisp and legible.

## 6. Text and Typography

Use text only as a supporting design element. Do not add a large headline.

Small numbers, letters, species names, short phrases, or index labels may be used.

Use restrained Swiss modernist typography, with fine annotations reminiscent of archival records, art publications, or experimental design journals.

## 7. Final Visual Requirements

Aim for the level of visual identity associated with arts institutions, architecture practices, experimental design studios, and high-end brands.

Give the work a **minimal, precise, restrained, and independent character, with a sense of cultural memory and a coherent visual system**.

Distill the photograph into an original, recognizable abstract visual identity with depth.

Maintain a **3:4 portrait format with two equal halves**. Do not add borders, additional photographs, collages, or decorative panels.
